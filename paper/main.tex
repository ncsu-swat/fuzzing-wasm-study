\begin{abstract}
\sloppy
WebAssembly (WASM) is gaining traction as a portable, high-performance, and secure execution technology for its ability to deliver near-native speed across platforms. WASM runtimes are optimized systems capable of executing binary WASM modules. As complex software, WASM runtimes contain bugs. Fuzzing is a popular approach to finding bugs in these systems, but an empirical study involving three popular WebAssembly runtimes, existing fuzzers, and existing seed corpora suggests that the bug-revealing ability of fuzzers is increasingly constrained by seed corpora that fail to keep pace with the evolving WebAssembly ecosystem.

This paper reports on a large-scale empirical study of regression test reuse for WebAssembly runtimes. We observe that independently developed runtimes adopt standardized WebAssembly proposals asynchronously, causing semantic corner cases discovered in one runtime to remain relevant to others. Motivated by this observation, we investigate two complementary forms of regression test reuse: (1) transplanting regression tests across runtimes and (2) using regression tests as seed corpora for fuzzing. Our study revealed \textbf{\TotalBugs{}} previously unknown bugs across three WASM runtimes, including \textbf{\TransplantationBugsCount{}} through transplantation and \textbf{\FuzzingBugsCount{}} through fuzzing, substantially exceeding the number of bugs found using well-known seed corpora. Our findings provide empirical evidence that regression tests constitute an effective testing resource for independently evolving WebAssembly runtimes and yield actionable recommendations for future testing.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\sloppy
WebAssembly (\wasm{}) is emerging as a portable, high-performance, and secure execution technology. Originally designed for web browsers, it has evolved into a general-purpose compilation target for languages such as C, C++, and Rust. \wasm{} delivers near-native speed while providing strong security guarantees and consistent behavior across platforms. \wasm{} runtimes (\eg{},  \wasmtime{}~\cite{wasmtime}, \wasmer{}~\cite{wasmer}, \wamr{}~\cite{wamr}, and \wasmedge{}~\cite{wasmedge}) are responsible for loading, verifying, and executing \wasm{} binaries. These systems are highly optimized and widely deployed, powering applications that range from serverless and edge computing to embedded systems. However, like any complex software, \wasm{} runtimes are prone to bugs. Their components such as interpreters, JIT compilers, and sandboxing layers can harbor subtle correctness, performance, and security issues. Such bugs may lead to crashes, miscompilations, or vulnerabilities. As \wasm{} adoption grows, ensuring the robustness and correctness of its runtimes becomes critical. 
% Understanding the nature and prevalence of bugs in these systems, and 
Developing effective methods to uncover and fix bugs in \wasm{} runtimes is therefore an important research challenge.

Several fuzzers have been proposed to detect bugs in \wasm{} runtimes, \eg{}, \wadiff{}~\cite{zhou2023wadiff}, \wapplique{}~\cite{zhao2024wapplique}, \wasmaker{}~\cite{cao2024wasmaker}, \added[id=ishrak]{and \lwdiff{}~\cite{zhou2025lwdiff}}. These tools have previously uncovered many inconsistencies, crashes, and specification deviations across runtime implementations. However, no prior work has studied the continued effectiveness of these fuzzers when used with the existing seed corpora. As observed across various domains~\cite{miller1990empirical,xu2020krace,lyu2018smartseed,bohme2017markov,manes2019survey}, fuzzing effectiveness heavily depends on the quality and diversity of the input corpus. The quality and diversity of an outdated corpus may degrade over time as its effectiveness becomes saturated; thereby leading to limited coverage and fewer bugs found, whereas a well-curated corpus from a new source can substantially extend the effectiveness and relevance of existing fuzzers. Understanding how corpus quality influences \wasm{} fuzzing outcomes remains a crucial step toward more systematic and effective testing of \wasm{} runtimes.

Bugs in software systems tend to concentrate according to the Pareto distribution \cite{huang2013evaluation}, which is why repeated bug fixes are usually spatially localized \cite{yue2017characterization}. Intuitively, regression tests (\ie{}, that previously revealed bugs) are rich in features, they have high potential to reveal new bugs. Based on this conjecture, \textbf{this paper presents a large-scale empirical study on how regression tests can be used to reveal new bugs in \wasm{} runtimes}. Specifically, we examine the effectiveness of two complementary approaches leveraging regression tests to find bugs in \wasm{} runtimes: (1)~\emph{test transplantation}, where regression tests are executed across different runtimes, and (2)~\emph{fuzzing} with a seed corpus of regression tests. Although regression tests have been used before for test generation \cite{plein2024automatic, rabin2021configuring}, we are the first to systematically study the effectiveness of regression tests to find new bugs in the WebAssembly domain using both test transplantation and fuzzing. 
\added[id=ishrak]{We hypothesize that regression tests are particularly valuable in the WebAssembly ecosystem because independent runtimes adopt new specification proposals asynchronously, allowing semantic corner cases captured in one implementation to remain effective in exposing analogous defects in others.}


To support our study, we introduce \rtbench{}, a dataset of 584 WebAssembly regression tests collected from multiple runtimes that we use for transplantation and as seeds for fuzzing. Our results show that the two bug-finding mechanisms we employ 
% regression test transplantation and fuzzing with regression tests as seeds 
are complementary in revealing bugs in \wasm{} runtimes. Transplanting regression tests uncovered \textbf{\TransplantationBugsCount{}} previously unknown bugs which is a modest but significant number, when compared to the 10 bugs found by the dedicated fuzzer \wapplique{} after several hours of fuzzing. Using \rtbench{} as a seed corpus markedly improved fuzzing effectiveness: fuzzers achieved higher coverage and exposed \textbf{\FuzzingBugsCount{}} additional bugs compared to three existing seed corpora used in the literature. Importantly, none of the bugs discovered through transplantation overlapped with those found by fuzzing, highlighting their complementary nature. The detected issues spanned compilation and runtime failures, including crashes and output mismatches. Notably, the most effective seeds originated from regression tests created two to three years ago, coinciding with the transition from WebAssembly version 1 to 2, while older tests, though less useful for fuzzing, continued to reveal unique issues through transplantation.

\subsection{WebAssembly Module}
\label{sec:background_module}

WebAssembly (\wasm{}) is a virtual instruction set architecture that is often used as a compilation target for various high-level languages. A \wasm{} module is the fundamental unit of deployment and execution; it is a platform-independent binary containing functions, type definitions, and data. WebAssembly Text (WAT) format~\cite{spec_wat} is a textual representation of a \wasm{} file. Decompilers from \wasm{} to WAT exist \cite{wasm_to_wat, wasm_tools}.

\subsection{WebAssembly Runtimes}
\label{sec:background_runtimes}
A WebAssembly runtime is a stack-based virtual machine that provides an execution model to load, instantiate, and run programs that have been compiled as WASM binary modules~\cite{zhang2024research}. A WASM runtime processes a module in three stages: (1) decoding, (2) validation, and (3) execution. Similar to a compiler, decoding converts the binary into an intermediate representation or directly into machine code. Validation ensures consistency of the types and the operand stack \cite{spec_concepts}. Execution takes place by directly invoking the \CodeIn{start} function, if the module defines one, 
or by loading the module, possibly from a different programming language or from the Command Line Interface (\CodeIn{CLI}) and calling exported functions.

A WebAssembly runtime maintains three stacks: an operand stack to track instruction operands, a control stack to track control flow, and an initialization stack to track local variables of a function \cite{spec_algo}. There are two ways to execute a \wasm{} module: (1) by executing directly on standalone WebAssembly runtimes that run on an Operating System \cite{zhang2024research} and (2) by embedding a \wasm{} module inside a host application (e.g. a browser) with the necessary glue code and programmatic API access to the WebAssembly runtimes. This paper focuses on the standalone versions of the WebAssembly runtimes where we directly use their \CodeIn{CLI} to execute \wasm{} modules.

\subsection{Mutational Fuzzing of WASM Runtimes}
\label{sec:background_fuzzers}

Several domain-specific fuzzers have recently been proposed to assess the robustness and security of WASM runtimes~\cite{wasmsmith, zhou2023wadiff, zhao2024wapplique, cao2024wasmaker, zhou2025lwdiff}. Most of these fuzzers are \emph{mutational}~\cite{zhou2023wadiff, zhao2024wapplique, cao2024wasmaker, zhou2025lwdiff}, \ie{}, they generate new inputs through systematic mutations of existing inputs as opposed to creating new inputs. Typically, a mutational fuzzer initializes a population with a \emph{seed corpus} and iteratively applies mutation operators to individuals from the population. In WASM mutational fuzzing, mutation operators can be applied at a low- or high-level data representation. More precisely, mutation operators can either modify WASM binary files or their corresponding abstract syntax trees (ASTs). For determining test outcomes, fuzzers typically rely on oracles that identify crashes or differential output among multiple runtimes.

As observed across multiple software domains, the quality and diversity of the seed corpus play a crucial role in determining the effectiveness of fuzzing~\cite{miller1990empirical,xu2020krace,lyu2018smartseed,bohme2017markov,manes2019survey}.

\subsection{\tname{}}
\label{sec:rtbench}

We propose \tname{}, a seed corpus of \wasm{} files mined from bug histories and regression tests. To construct our corpus, we collected seeds from real-world bug reports spanning a five-year period from 2020 to 2025. We mined the public GitHub issue trackers and regression testsuites of the four major WebAssembly runtimes, namely \wasmtime{}~\cite{wasmtime}, \wasmer{}~\cite{wasmer}, \wamr{}~\cite{wamr}, and \wasmedge{}~\cite{wasmedge} to collect \rtbenchseedcount{} \wasm{} files. The distribution of the collected seed testcases are shown in Table \ref{tab:seed_distribution}.

\input{tables/rtbench_distribution}

We focus on issues that refer to either crashing inputs or the ones leading to differential output. For each candidate, we first identify the buggy runtime version from the issue report. We then execute the test case on that version to confirm bug reproducibility. Importantly, we also verify that none of these seeds reproduce crashes on the latest versions of the corresponding runtimes at the time of our study, confirming that the underlying bugs have been fixed. This approach provided us with a seed corpus grounded in actual historical bugs while ensuring they had indeed been buggy in the reported versions and that they no longer manifest in current releases. These checks help to form a realistic and reliable seed corpus for subsequent fuzzing and cross-runtime differential testing.

When collecting test cases from the runtime testsuites, we only select the \CodeIn{.wasm} files since the fuzzers expect only \CodeIn{.wasm} files as seeds (\textsection{}\ref{sec:background_fuzzers}). For testcases collected from issues, we only keep the ones related to \CodeIn{CLI} bugs of the runtimes (\textsection{}\ref{sec:background_runtimes}). For testcases collected from both issues and testsuites, we filter out duplicates and non-deterministic cases.


\subsection{Fuzzer Provenance Logging}
\label{sec:provenance_logging}

For any large-scale fuzzing campaign, it is essential not only to generate diverse test cases but also to maintain a clear record of how those test cases were derived. As discussed at Section~\ref{sec:background_fuzzers}, the two mutation-based fuzzers used in our study, \wapplique{}~\cite{zhao2024wapplique} and \wasmaker{}~\cite{cao2024wasmaker}, generate new \wasm{} modules by splicing one or more existing \wasm{} modules. To ensure reproducibility and accountability across this process, we extended both fuzzers with a lightweight provenance logging mechanism that records the original seeds, the fragments used, and the mutation operations applied.

In \wasmaker{}~\cite{cao2024wasmaker}, seed \wasm{} binaries are parsed into per-function abstract syntax trees (ASTs), analyzed at the opcode level, and then mutated to form new inputs. We integrated provenance logging at the AST generation stage.
Similarly, \wapplique{}~\cite{zhao2024wapplique} decomposes real \wasm{} seeds into reusable code fragments from function bodies, which are then recombined and slightly mutated to synthesize new test cases. 
To ensure that these instrumentation changes did not alter fuzzer correctness or efficiency, we validated the modified fuzzers against their original behavior. Both fuzzers produced the same ratio of valid to invalid test cases as their uninstrumented versions. The overhead of provenance logging was negligible 
This validation confirmed that our logging mechanism maintained functional equivalence while adding transparent, low-cost traceability to our fuzzing campaigns.

\subsection{Differential testing}
\label{sec:diff_testing}
To systematically uncover inconsistencies across WebAssembly runtimes, we developed a large-scale differential testing framework that executes the same input \wasm{} file under multiple runtime configurations and compares their outcomes. When running each input \wasm{} file, we invoke all of the exported functions on different modes of the runtimes.

\subsubsection{Runtime Modes.} Our differential testing framework supports a variety of execution and compilation modes across the tested runtimes. For \wasmtime{}, both direct interpretation and execution of precompiled (\CodeIn{.cwasm}) artifacts are tested. \wasmer{} is evaluated under its \CodeIn{Cranelift} and \CodeIn{LLVM} backends. \wamr{} is exercised through its ahead-of-time compiler \CodeIn{wamrc}, native execution in AOT mode, interpreter, and JIT mode using the \CodeIn{iwasm} application. \wasmedge{} is tested in JIT mode and interpreter mode with the \CodeIn{wasmedge} application, and compiled mode with \CodeIn{wasmedgec} compiler. Each run is wrapped within a 10-second timeout to detect hangs, ensuring the module can capture various types of runtime behaviors while avoiding indefinite stalls. This layered design provides a robust methodology for discovering cross-runtime semantic mismatches and exposing previously unknown bugs.

\subsubsection{Output Normalization.} To minimize false positives and capture semantic output differences instead of just cosmetic divergences, we have developed a postprocessing layer which normalizes runtime outputs and maps diverse error messages to standardized categories. Each runtime (\wasmtime{}, \wasmer{}, \wamr{}, and \wasmedge{}) has a dedicated postprocessor function that detects recurring failure modes such as \CodeIn{out\_of\_bounds}, \CodeIn{integer\_divide\_by\_zero}, \CodeIn{invalid\_func\_nargs}, or \CodeIn{unsupported}. Outputs that involve floating-point are normalized into consistent mantissa–exponent representations, and +/- `NaN'/`nan' literals are standardized to ensure comparability. By filtering away runtime-specific warnings, trimming extraneous logs, and unifying divergent error messages, the module isolates semantically meaningful differences. This guarantees that any observed discrepancies across runtimes reflect true semantic divergences rather than cosmetic variations in error reporting.


\subsection{Triaging}
After identifying likely anomalies, we triage these cases by deduplication and simplification which are elaborated below.
\label{sec:triaging}
\subsubsection{Deduplication} To efficiently triage recurring crashes across WebAssembly runtimes, we implemented a crash stack trace deduplication module that couples a Python callback in LLDB \cite{lldb} with a Bash wrapper for automated execution. The wrapper script launches the program under LLDB in batch mode, installs a custom crash hook, and configures signal handling for \CodeIn{SIGSEGV} and \CodeIn{SIGABRT}. For Rust-based runtimes, we configured the panic mode to abort. When such abnormal terminations occur, the Python hook records the top five frames of the crashing thread, including function names and source file locations, filtered by a configurable path pattern. These frames are appended to a trace file and act as a concise fingerprint of the failure. By grouping test cases with identical top-frame sequences, the module coarsely clusters crashes that share the same underlying defect while avoiding over-fragmentation due to deeper stack variations. This design reduces redundant triage effort by collapsing large sets of failing test cases into representative clusters that can be more thoroughly inspected.

To triage differential outputs observed across runtimes, we built an execution tracing module that conditions on the divergent output itself. The module leverages LLDB to perform instruction-level tracing of a target program until a specified output string appears on stdout. When a mismatch is detected between runtimes, the expected differential output is supplied as the tracing target, and execution halts as soon as this output is observed. At each step, the tracer records the current function, file, and line number, producing a detailed trace of the program’s execution path leading to the discrepancy. Since such traces can be verbose and include incidental logging, we focus on the most recent fifteen frames preceding the divergent output as a fingerprint for deduplication. By comparing these condensed trace signatures, the module distinguishes whether two test cases that generate the same output pattern actually expose the same underlying bug or separate issues. This approach improves the efficiency of triaging differential outputs.

\subsubsection{Test Reduction} Before reporting bugs, we apply automated test case reduction to minimize failing inputs while preserving the triggering behavior. This is handled through a reducer module built around the \CodeIn{wasm-tools~shrink} utility, which iteratively simplifies WebAssembly binaries under the guidance of a custom predicate. The predicate runs the reduced module through our differential test engine and deduplication pipeline, compares the produced outputs against the reference failing outputs, and accepts the minimized candidate only if the failure is preserved. This ensures that irrelevant instructions and sections are pruned away, leaving behind the smallest input that still reproduces the crash or differential output. The module produces both a minimized \CodeIn{.wasm} file and a corresponding textual \CodeIn{.wat} representation for inspection.

By combining various deduplication strategies with a systematic reduction, we prepared the final list of bugs and test cases for reporting to the corresponding runtime issue trackers.

We observe that regression tests from all runtimes except \wasmer{}, could reveal new bugs in the other runtimes, but the effect is uneven. Overall, these results indicate that test transplantation is capable of uncovering previously-hidden bugs in runtimes.

Figure \ref{ex:rq2_ex1} shows one such bug found in the \wasmedge{} runtime by transplanting the test \href{https://github.com/bytecodealliance/wasm-micro-runtime/issues/3391}{WAMR\#3391}. During execution, the null value is interpreted, triggering a crash. Thus, the user is not responsible for explicitly initializing \CodeIn{externref} variables to \CodeIn{ref.null}. Hence, the absence of default initialization in \wasmedge{} risked an eventual null pointer dereference. This bug was fixed by the pull request (\href{https://github.com/WasmEdge/WasmEdge/pull/4329}{PR\#4329}) shortly after reporting.

The top-four horizontal lines from Figure \ref{fig:transplantation_timeline} indicate the timelines associated with the origin of the test cases whereas the bottom-four horizontal lines indicate the timelines for revealing a bug with transplantation, \ie{}, the time a bug would be revealed for the first time in a different runtime. As we did not find any bugs on the \wasmtime{} runtime, the revelation bar of \wasmtime{} has been grayed out. 

The visualization reveals two patterns. 
First, \TransplantationPreExistingCount{} of \TransplantationBugsCount{} bugs (i.e. \TransplantationPreExistingPercentage{}) existed long before their official reports (i.e., T1, T3, T7-10, and T13).

In both cases, transplantation of regression tests across runtimes is beneficial. The benefit would be immediate in the first case and would be delayed in the latter.
Figure \ref{fig:transplantation_timeline} also shows that most transplantation bugs cluster around 2022--2024, which was a period coinciding with major WebAssembly specification updates \cite{changelog1, changelog2}.

\subsection{\textbf{RQ4: \rqfour{}{}}}
\label{sec:rqfour}

Table \ref{tab:error_conditions} characterizes representative bugs revealed with \tname{} as the seed corpus. These bugs are categorized by using terminology consistent with prior WASM testing work \cite{zhao2024wapplique}, \cite{cao2024wasmaker}. We group them into two main categories: \CodeIn{runtime~failure} and  \CodeIn{compilation~failure}. Runtime failures refer to crashes that occur during execution, which we further distinguish by the type of exception. A compilation failure occurs when a Wasm binary cannot be successfully compiled or instantiated, \ie{} it fails before execution begins.

We further categorize bugs based on their symptoms. Each testcase’s output was analyzed to determine whether it belongs to the \CodeIn{Crash} or \CodeIn{Output-difference} category. If a testcase triggers a runtime crash (e.g., \CodeIn{segfault} or \CodeIn{aborted}), it is placed under the crash category; if it produces inconsistent outputs across runtimes, it is classified as an output-difference bug. The categorization process begins by minimizing each testcase with \CodeIn{wasm-tools~shrink} to its smallest reproducible form, then identifying the specific code segment responsible for the behavior. To validate our classification, we modified and relocated the identified code segments to confirm that they consistently reproduced the observed behavior. Finally, the structure of each minimized code pattern guided its placement into the most relevant category.

For instance, one representative runtime failure (Row 5) involves a recursive call inside a vector-returning function that never terminates and is eventually followed by an \CodeIn{unreachable} instruction, leading the runtime into an infinite loop before crashing. Another case (Row 11) represents a compilation failure where the compiler miscomputes lane extraction as an \CodeIn{i32} address and then incorrectly uses this address as the base for a SIMD load \CodeIn{v128.load8\_splat}, triggering a validation error during instantiation.

The following examples illustrate \deleted[id=ishrak]{two test cases that demonstrate a compilation failure and a runtime failure, respectively} \added[id=ishrak]{three test cases related to different kinds of failures}. Each case highlights the key instruction patterns and runtime behaviors that led to the observed failures.

Figure~\ref{example:bug_examples}(a) reveals a crash pattern linked to incorrect handling of multi-value function returns involving SIMD vector types. 
The critical lines are \CodeIn{(type~(func~(result~i32~i32~v128)))} and the instruction sequence \CodeIn{i32.const~0} $\rightarrow$ \CodeIn{i32.const~0} $\rightarrow$ \CodeIn{v128.const~i32x4~0~0~0~0}. 
Here, the function is declared to return two \CodeIn{i32} values followed by a \CodeIn{v128} vector. 
While this is a valid multi-value return signature under the WebAssembly SIMD and multi-value proposals, certain runtimes, particularly LLVM-based JIT backends mishandle the lowering of mixed scalar and SIMD return values. 
During compilation, the LLVM backend emits mismatched IR types between the declared function signature and the actual return instruction (e.g., returning \CodeIn{i64,~i128} instead of the expected multi-value tuple), leading to an internal assertion failure ``\texttt{Function return type does not match operand type of return inst}''. 
This results in a compiler abort rather than a clean trap. 
Therefore, the failure is classified as a \CodeIn{Compilation~Failure~type-mismatch~bug}, specifically triggered by the combination of multi-value returns and SIMD vector types that cause inconsistencies in function return type lowering during LLVM IR generation.

Figure~\ref{example:bug_examples}(b) demonstrates a runtime crash caused by incorrect handling of typed references within control-flow boundaries. 
The instruction sequence \CodeIn{br 0} $\rightarrow$ \CodeIn{ref.null 0} is responsible for triggering the failure. 
The \CodeIn{br 0} instruction transfers control to the nearest enclosing block, rendering subsequent code unreachable, yet the engine must still correctly validate or lower it. 
Immediately following this, the instruction \CodeIn{ref.null 0} attempts to create a null reference of heap type index~\CodeIn{0}, which corresponds to the first function type defined in the module~(\CodeIn{type (func)}). 
While this construct is syntactically valid under the reference-types proposal, certain runtimes particularly JIT-based ones mishandle the combination of an unreachable code region with a typed reference operand, leading to inconsistent type-state tracking or invalid IR generation during lowering. 
This results in a runtime failure, specifically in the runtime’s implementation of reference-type validation and unreachable-block handling.

\textcolor{orange}{Figure~\ref{example:bug_examples}(c) shows a core execution bug during runtime. The test case module has a 1-page memory (max 2) and an active data segment with empty contents at offset 896340315, far beyond the memory’s size. Under bulk-memory semantics, an out-of-bounds active segment should result in a handled trap at instantiation even when empty. But in this case this test case resulted in an unhandled segmentation fault.}

\added[id=mohsen]{In Table~\ref{tab:proposal_technique}, we characterized all reported bugs by proposal and by discovery technique. Two patterns stand out. All Reference-Types and GC bugs (3 of 3) were found only through transplantation. These bugs share a common cause: runtimes omit an implicit specification obligation, such as default-initializing an \CodeIn{externref} to \CodeIn{ref.null}, validating a typed-reference operand, or rejecting an illegal \CodeIn{ref eq} type constructor. A fuzzer's mutation operators rarely construct these cases, since the bug lies in an absent or misused construct rather than an executable instruction pattern. Conversely, all 6 bugs tied to the core execution engine rather than a specific proposal, including call-stack depth limits, trap and signal handling, and parser edge cases, were found only through fuzzing. SIMD is the largest category overall (14 bugs) but leans heavily toward fuzzing, which found 12 of them versus 2 from transplantation. WASI/host-interface bugs (5) were split between the techniques. These results show that transplantation and fuzzing (\textsection{}\ref{sec:rqthree}); target different classes of bugs.}


\begin{tcolorbox}[rqbox]
\emph{\textbf{Summary of RQ4.}}~SIMD/Multi-value accounts for the largest share of bugs found with \tname{} (14 of \TotalBugs{}), predominantly via fuzzing (12 of 14). Reference-Types/GC bugs (3) were found exclusively through transplantation, while core-execution-engine bugs (6) were found exclusively through fuzzing, evidence that the two techniques target structurally different classes of bugs.

\end{tcolorbox}

\label{sec:rqfive}

Figure \ref{fig:testcase_lineage} highlights several important patterns in bug propagation from seeds to new test cases across different WebAssembly runtimes. First, seeds from \wasmtime{} and \wamr{} dominate the flow, producing a substantial number of downstream bugs, whereas \wasmer{} and \wasmedge{} appear more frequently as buggy runtimes than as primary seed sources.

\end{tcolorbox}

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

This section presents lessons learned and threats to validity.

\subsection{Lessons Learned}
\label{sec:lessons}

We summarize key lessons learned as a set of observations and corresponding recommendations.

\begin{enumerate}[
    itemsep=0pt,
    parsep=0pt,
    topsep=2pt,
    partopsep=0pt
]

\item \added[id=ishrak]{\textbf{Reuse Regression Tests Directly on Asynchronously Evolving Implementations}~Although WebAssembly specifies standardized semantics, major language proposals (e.g., Reference Types, SIMD, Exception Handling, Memory64, and GC) are implemented by runtimes at different times depending on implementation priorities and available resources. Consequently, regression tests that expose corner cases in an early adopter frequently remain effective for revealing analogous implementation defects in later adopters. This asynchronous proposal-adoption dynamic explains why regression tests remain valuable across independently developed runtimes and motivates the use of cross-runtime regression-test transplantation.} \added[id=mohsen]{This is borne out empirically: every Reference-Types/GC bug in our study was found through transplantation and none through fuzzing (\textsection{}\ref{sec:rqfour}), directly supporting this asynchronous-adoption hypothesis.}

\item \textbf{Monitor all runtimes and integrate new regression tests.}~Our study shows that regression tests from one runtime can directly reveal bugs in others without additional overhead and also serve as effective seeds for fuzzing (\textsection{}\ref{sec:rqone} \& \ref{sec:rqtwo}). Moreover, \TransplantationPreExistingPercentage{} of the bugs found by transplantation existed long before in the observed runtimes (\textsection{}\ref{sec:rqone}). So, we recommend the integration of regression tests as soon as they are updated.

\item \textbf{Prioritize regression tests created during major specification upgrades.}~We observed that 90\% of the useful transplanted tests and fuzzing seeds originated during major specification upgrades from version 1 to 2 (\textsection{}\ref{sec:rqone} \& \ref{sec:rqfive}) \cite{changelog1, changelog2}. Specification upgrades are routine and version 3 has been recently finalized \cite{changelog3}. Therefore, as implementations for version 3 start rolling out, we recommend automated test generation from the spec updates similar to prior work on spec-based test generation \cite{rao2024diffspec}.

\item \textbf{Continuously update and maintain \tname{}.}~The long-term utility of \tname{} depends on both its evolution and its curation. As new regression tests are introduced to fix bugs or cover new specification features, they should be promptly incorporated into \tname{} to capture up-to-date runtime behavior. We also found that older seeds were largely ineffective for both transplantation and fuzzing (\textsection{}\ref{sec:rqfive}). So, older seeds exercising stable features or redundant paths should be periodically retired for the efficiency of \tname{} seed corpora \cite{li2025improving, rebert2014optimizing, herrera2021seed}.

\item \textbf{Adjust mutation strategy based on seed complexity.}
Our results indicate that mutation effectiveness of \wasm{} fuzzers is tied to seed complexity. We observed that \wadiff{}'s havoc mutations are effective on only \specbench{} corpora (\textsection{}\ref{sec:rqtwo}). It is worth noting that \specbench{} contains simple and focused seeds (\textsection{}\ref{sec:seed_corpora}). In contrast, \wasmaker{} and \wapplique{}’s splicing mutations perform well only when applied to complex and diverse seed corpora such as \rtbench{}~(\textsection{}\ref{sec:rqtwo}, \textsection{}\ref{sec:seed_corpora}). Based on our observations, we recommend a seed-complexity aware mutation strategy i.e. to apply havoc mutations on simple seeds and splicing on complex ones for \wasm{} fuzzing.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Case study: Why does \rtbench{} overcome saturation?}
\label{sec:overcoming_saturation}
\added[id=ishrak]{To better understand why \rtbench{} is able to overcome the saturation observed with existing seed corpora, we compared the line-level coverage of \rtbench{} and \wasmbench{} on the \wasmer{} runtime. We selected this case because \rtbench{} exhibits one of the largest initial coverage advantage over \wasmbench{} in Table \ref{tab:seed_corpora}. The comparison reveals that the additional coverage is not concentrated in simply instruction execution alone, but in runtime infrastructure responsible for instance management, VM context handling, memory management, trap handling, and runtime library calls. In particular, \rtbench{} uniquely exercises code for memory initialization, copy, fill, growth, table initialization, data segment operations, imported and local memory resolution, and VM context manipulation, many of which remain completely uncovered when fuzzing starts from \wasmbench{}.}

\added[id=ishrak]{These results suggest that \rtbench{}'s advantage stems from exposing fuzzing to diverse instructions and a broader set of runtime states and execution contexts, enabling mutations to continue exploring previously unreachable behaviors after conventional seed corpora have saturated.}

\subsection{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:threats}

\added[id=mohsen]{\noindent \textbf{Construct.}~A potential threat concerns how we define and count bugs. Some anomalies could stem from undefined runtime behaviors. Although our differential testing module normalizes outputs, nondeterministic effects may still yield spurious mismatches. To mitigate this, we identified patterns of known undefined behaviors and semi-automatically filtered such cases. Our proposal-level classification of the reported bugs (\textsection{}\ref{sec:rqfour}) was performed manually against each bug's minimized test case and error output; a second author independently reviewed the classification to reduce subjectivity, with disagreements resolved by discussion.} 
\noindent \textbf{Internal.}~Fuzzing inherently involves randomness, which can affect coverage and bug counts. To mitigate randomness bias, we executed each fuzzing campaign five times under fixed time budgets and identical configurations, then aggregated unique bug outcomes across runs. \textcolor{orange}{Additionally, we evaluate the corpus as a whole without showing exactly what properties of a seed drive gains. We partially mitigate this threat with a post-hoc mapping of bugs to WebAssembly proposals (Table \ref{tab:proposal_technique}). In both transplantation and fuzzing, we found that the presence of proposal-specific instructions is associated with effective seeds. We will analyze other seeds properties and their interaction in future work.}
\noindent \textbf{External.}~Another threat relates to the representativeness of our objects of study i.e. runtimes, fuzzers, and seed corpora. We focused on four mature and \fuzzercount{} widely used mutational fuzzers, which may not generalize to emerging engines or generative fuzzers. Similarly, the \tname{} corpus was derived from regression tests of these four runtimes and may underrepresent other WebAssembly ecosystems. We mitigate this by selecting the most widely used runtimes, fuzzers, and seed corpora from prior work, and by releasing \tname{} to enable extension in future studies.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

This paper presents \tname{}, a dataset of real-world \wasm{} regression tests, and reports on an in-depth evaluation of two complementary strategies (namely, test transplantation and regression-seeded fuzzing) for uncovering bugs in WASM runtimes. Across three major runtimes, we discovered \TotalBugs{} bugs, with transplantation and fuzzing revealing distinct and non-overlapping issues. Our results show that regression tests remain valuable long after their creation to assist bug-fixes, especially those created during major specification updates.

Overall, results indicate that the use of project metadata, such as issues and release notes, is a promising direction to improve effectiveness of WASM fuzzers.
